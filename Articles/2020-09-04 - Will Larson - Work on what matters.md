# Work on what matters

[Read the guide](https://staffeng.com/guides/work-on-what-matters/).

## Summary

Larson argues that increasing expectations and finite time make work selection central to sustained senior engineering practice. He names three failure modes:

- **Snacking:** choosing easy, low-impact tasks for their quick sense of accomplishment when harder, consequential work is available.
- **Preening:** choosing visible but low-impact work because an organisation rewards the appearance of leadership.
- **Chasing ghosts:** importing a diagnosis or strategy from a previous context before understanding the present organisation.

His positive sequence starts with existential risks: when organisational survival or basic system viability is threatened, efficiency of contribution is secondary. Outside those moments, he recommends consequential areas with enough room to contribute and enough organisational attention to sustain the result. Important work that the organisation persistently devalues can produce early gains that later erode; Larson argues that senior engineers should recognise this constraint while retaining an ethical obligation to advocate for some neglected concerns.

Larson identifies four other forms of leverage. Developing colleagues through onboarding, mentoring, and coaching compounds beyond an individual's output. "Editing" means using experience and relationships for a small intervention that materially improves another project. Helping rescope or unblock nearly complete work turns effort into a usable result. Finally, senior engineers should reserve attention for valuable work that depends on their distinctive capability or commitment and otherwise may not happen.

## Perspective and limits

This is practitioner guidance from a book chapter, supported by reasoning, personal experience, and linked examples rather than comparative or outcome research. It does not define a measurement method for impact or show that the proposed prioritisation improves organisational results. Career levels, influence, organisational privilege, and freedom to select work vary by company.

Organisational attention is an imperfect signal: the article itself notes that essential work such as developer tooling, inclusion, and glue work may be systematically undervalued. Completion also should not be treated as the only source of value; learning, risk reduction, maintenance, and a well-supported decision to stop can matter even when a project does not ship. The categories are therefore prompts for judgment, not a universal ranking formula.

## Staff synthesis

[[Staff Engineer]] uses this source to add work selection before problem framing and system design. The useful distinction is not simply easy versus hard work. Staff-level leverage requires judging consequence, tractability, organisational support, opportunity cost, and whether the engineer's experience or relationships change the likely outcome.

## FDE transfer

The article is not about forward deployed engineering. For a Staff-level FDE, its bounded transfer is to distinguish a visible or urgent customer request from consequential work, then identify where field context, technical judgment, or cross-team relationships can unblock a valuable result. Engagement scope, customer authority, product decision rights, and sustainable ownership still constrain the intervention; "work only you can do" should not create dependence on one field engineer.
